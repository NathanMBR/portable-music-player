# Portable Music Player UI

This Storybook is the detailed documentation of the player's interface. Every component has a
`*.stories.tsx` file next to it. Its Docs page describes what the component is for, lists its props
(read from the JSDoc in the component's source) and shows one story per situation it handles. The
**Design tokens** page covers colors, type and interaction states.

## The app shell

`App` fills the window with the view of the current category. `App` maps each category id to its
view (`viewByCategory`), so the navigation feature never imports the views it switches between.

- **Margins:** the view keeps equal side margins, sized by the `gutter` token (`px-gutter`). The
  menu button sits in the left one, so views never include it and nothing scrolls under it.
- **Scrolling:** the window itself never scrolls. A view keeps its header in place and scrolls
  only the content below it. The app draws its own scrollbar, in the right margin beside the
  content, and the content keeps the width between the margins. The content reaches the window's
  bottom edge, or ends above the player while it's open.

The **audio player** (`AudioPlayer`) slides up along the bottom of the window when a song is played,
and away when it's stopped. `App` renders it outside the views, so a song keeps playing when the
category changes. It holds shuffle, previous, play or pause, stop, next and repeat on the left, the
song's title and artist, as in the song list, above its progress bar in the middle, and the speaker
and volume on the right. For a song played from a playlist, the playlist's title follows the
artist, behind another middle dot, in the artist's type and ink. While the song plays, the name
slides from right to left without end (`Marquee`), from the start of the left time's digits to the
end of the length's, and comes in again from the right one second after it starts going off on the
left; while it's paused, the name holds still in the middle. The bar has the elapsed
time on its left, which a click turns into the time left counted down, and the song's length on its right. The progress stops growing at a set
width, so in wide windows the spaces around it widen instead.
Clicking the speaker mutes, and clicking it again brings the volume back, and the mouse wheel over
the volume turns it up or down by 5%. The song plays at the volume slider's value as it's dragged,
but the volume is chosen only once it's released: released at zero, it mutes, so unmuting brings
back the volume from before the drag. The views end above its space, and while it's open
notifications pop up above it, both sized by the `player` token. Both follow its slide: notifications
on screen as it opens or closes move with it. The progress bar is a slider:
clicking or dragging it jumps within the song. With the mouse over it, a small tooltip
(`Tooltip`) above it shows at once the time a click there would jump to, to the tenth of a second,
and the volume shows the volume a click would set the same way. Past a song's first 3 seconds,
previous starts it over. A song's length shows as `--:--` while its audio loads, and when the last
song ends without repeat, it stays in the player, paused at its start.

The player picks up where it was left: its volume, mute, repeat and shuffle, and the song it held,
paused at the same moment, come back when the app opens again. They're saved in `settings.json` a
moment after they change, and when the window closes. The song is found again by its file name, so
one removed from the `Songs` folder is forgotten, and the player stays closed. Nothing saved yet
means full volume, unmuted, with repeat and shuffle off.

Files and folders dropped anywhere on the window add their songs to the `Songs` folder. While files
are dragged over the window, the page blurs behind frosted glass and a box with marching dashes
covers it (`SongDropOverlay`), saying to drop them there. A dialog (`SongDropDialog`) then spins a
loader while Rust finds the MP3s of the drop, subfolders included, and copies them flat into the
`Songs` folder, which is then scanned again. When some of their file names are taken, the dialog
first lists them all, in a box that scrolls when they're many, and asks whether to skip those
songs, to replace the songs already there, or to cancel.

The **notification area** (`NotificationArea`) sits in the bottom-right corner, above everything,
the side menu included. Notifications pop up there, close by themselves after a few seconds or
earlier with their × button, and pause while hovered. Some say what was just done, like a deleted
playlist, with an Undo button that takes it back.

The **side menu** is an overlay drawer (`SideMenu`) opened by the menu button. It lists the
categories from `src/features/navigation/categories.ts` and holds the theme toggle. Choosing a
category switches the view and closes the drawer. Adding a category takes one entry in `CATEGORIES`
and one view in `viewByCategory`.

Once a check finds a newer release, a dot (`NotificationDot`) on the menu button says the menu holds
it, and an Update option follows the categories, with the same dot. It opens a dialog
(`UpdateDialog`) with the new version's patch notes (`PatchNotes`, from Markdown) and two choices:
skip the update, which stops offering it and saves its version so the check at launch passes over
it (checking by hand from Settings still offers it), or download it. An installed build installs it in place; a portable build, an executable or
the AppImage, opens its download in the browser.

Every category's view is a `ViewLayout`: a `ViewHeader` with the category's icon and title stays in
place while the content below it scrolls. A page inside a category, like a playlist, has a back
button in the icon's place. Below the header's rule, a `StatusLine` says something about the view,
like how many songs it lists, and a view with nothing to list says so in its middle (`EmptyState`).
Lists, of songs or of playlists, are rows between thin rules (`ListRow`), tinted under the mouse.

**Songs** (`LibraryView`) has a header, and below its rule the song count (`LibraryStatus`), then
the song list, a folder in the middle of the view saying the Songs folder is empty (`EmptyLibrary`),
or a calm notice with "Try again" after a failed scan; without songs, the count is left out. Each
song starts with a `PlayButton`, which plays it in the audio player with the whole list as the
queue. Next comes a square miniature of the song's cover (`CoverThumbnail`), or a photo icon on the
`rule` color when it has none. Then comes its title and, when its tags name one, its artist, past a
middle dot. The dot and the artist are in a lighter ink (`ink-faded`) than the title, as in "Better
than You · Metallica". The row under the mouse is tinted, and the song in the player, playing or
paused, has its row on a pen tint with its title and artist in pen, like the current category in
the menu. A song shows as the one in the player only in the list it plays from: played from a
playlist, it looks like any other song here, and its play button plays it from the Songs view. The
songs are sorted as the Settings view says: by title, the default, or by filename.

**Playlists** (`PlaylistsView`) lists the playlists of the `Playlists` folder, sorted by title
(`PlaylistsOverview`), or, without any, says so in the middle of the view. Each row
(`PlaylistRow`) has a play button, the cover of its first song that has one, or the Playlists icon
when none does, then the playlist's title and description, and a click anywhere else on it opens
the playlist. At its right end, a menu (`ActionMenu`) holds Recheck songs while
songs are missing, Edit playlist, and Delete playlist, which asks first (`ConfirmDialog`), then
notifies with an Undo. The last scan of the `Songs` folder, the song list, is the app's one record
of which songs are there: while it lacks some of a playlist's songs, a red alert (`AlertMark`) takes
the place of the playlist's play buttons and its songs' are disabled. Playing a playlist first has
Rust look for its songs in the `Songs` folder, as does a recheck, from its row's menu or its page's
Recheck button (`RecheckButton`): if some are missing, nothing plays and a notification says how
many, its file remembers it, and when Rust finds other songs than the song list holds, the folder is
scanned again, so the Songs view and every playlist follow at once. An open playlist
(`PlaylistPage`) has its title in the header, with a back button, a
large play button that starts from its first song, and its songs below, each with its own play
button; a song the `Songs` folder lacks is listed under its file name in a lighter ink. Its song in
the player has the same pen tint as in the song list, but only when it plays from this playlist,
not from the Songs view or another playlist. The songs
(`PlaylistSongList`) move by dragging their Move handle, with the pointer or from the keyboard
(`SortableList`), each row shows its song's position after the handle, and each row ends with a
menu (`ActionMenu`). Its Change position opens a dialog (`ChangePositionDialog`) that places the
song before or after another, picked from a search that also finds positions; its Remove from
playlist asks first, then notifies with an Undo. A playlist is saved as soon as it changes. The
open playlist stays open while other pages are on screen.

On the list of playlists, a round + button (`NewPlaylistButton`) floats in the bottom-right
corner, slides up from below the window's bottom edge, from behind the player while it's open,
and keeps above the player and below the notifications. It opens the New playlist dialog
(`PlaylistDialog`), in two steps, whose tabs (`StepTabs`) show the one on screen: first Details,
a title, required, and a description (`TextField`), then, after Next, Songs, the songs of the
Songs folder, each with a `Checkbox`, found by a search over their title, artist and file name
that ignores case and accents. Back returns to the first step, keeping what was typed and picked.
Creating, the tabs themselves can't be clicked; editing, they go to their step too. The playlist keeps the picked songs in the list's order. The Edit button
of a playlist's page, before its large play button, and Edit playlist, in the menu of its row,
open the same dialog as "Edit playlist",
filled with the playlist's values: the songs still picked keep their place, and the newly picked
ones follow.

**Settings** (`SettingsView`) holds a switch to search automatically for updates, on by default,
which has the app look for a newer release each time it opens, with a button before it to check by
hand while it's off (`CheckForUpdatesButton`), and a choice to sort the songs by filename or by
title (`Select`). Each option is a card (`SettingCard`), its label at the left and its control at
the right. Each choice applies at once and is saved in `settings.json`; if saving fails, the choice
still applies and a notification says why.

## Where the code lives

- `src/components/`: feature-agnostic building blocks (`ActionMenu`, `AlertMark`, `Button`,
  `Checkbox`, `ConfirmDialog`, `EmptyState`, `IconButton`, `ListRow`, `Modal`, `Notice`,
  `RadioGroup`, `Select`, `Slider`, `SortableList`, `StatusLine`, `Switch`, `TextField`, `Tooltip`,
  `ViewHeader`, `ViewLayout`), built on Headless UI and styled with Tailwind, each in a folder of
  its own with its stories. `buttonStyles.ts`, beside the folders, holds the looks and states every
  button shares: the `quiet`, `outlined` and `filled` variants, the active look, focus and
  disabled, and the panel a button opens, like a select's list or a menu.
- `src/features/notifications/`: the notification area and `notify.ts`, the only module that calls
  Sonner, the toast library behind the notifications.
- `src/features/<feature>/`: everything one feature owns. Its types mirror the Rust structs,
  `<feature>Api.ts` is the only file that calls Rust, `<feature>Store.ts` is its Zustand store, and
  each of its components sits in a folder of its own with its stories.

## How data flows

A component reads its state from a Zustand store and calls the store's actions. The store calls its
feature's `*Api.ts`, which uses Tauri's `invoke()` to run a Rust command. The Rust commands read
and write the `Songs` and `Playlists` folders and the `.json` files next to the executable, so everything stays on the
flash drive.

The library scan reads each song's tags in Rust, and only its tags, since the drive may be slow. A
`Track`'s `title` is its title tag, or its file name without the extension when it has none, and
its `artist` is the artist tag, else the album artist, else `null`. A song whose tags are missing or
corrupt still shows up, under its file name. Every scan after the one at launch, like "Scan again",
ends with a success notification (`notifySuccess`) counting the songs found.

Covers are read apart from the scan, one song at a time, only for the rows on screen. A row's square
asks for its cover once it has stayed on screen for a moment, so the rows a fast scroll flies past
read nothing. `read_cover` sends the song's front cover, else its first picture, as raw bytes, or no
bytes when it has none, and `coverStore.ts` shows them through an object URL. The store keeps a
limited number of pictures, freeing the ones asked for longest ago, remembers every song found
without one, and forgets them all after every scan, so changed covers are read again. A playlist's
square reads the covers of its songs in its order, one after the other, until one has a cover.
Songs without a picture, with tags that can't be read, or with a picture the webview can't draw
show the photo icon. A cover Rust can't read at all, because
the song vanished or the drive was pulled, shows it too, and raises one "Couldn't load a cover"
notification, which every further failure replaces instead of stacking a copy per row.

Rust errors reach the frontend as their message string. The store that caught one calls
`notifyError(title, error)`: the title says what failed, and Rust's message, shown as it is, says
why. Raising the same failure again replaces the notification on screen instead of stacking a copy.
The view itself never shows red text: when a failure leaves it with nothing to show, like a failed
scan, it shows a calm `Notice` with what to do next.

Songs play through one more loop, in Rust rather than the webview, so one song follows the next
without a gap. `songEngine.ts`, connected once from `main.tsx`, is the only module that drives
Rust's player. It follows the playback store's song, play state, position and volume through
Rust's commands, and reports the song's length, progress and end back to the store, which decides
what plays next. Rust always holds that next song, decoded ahead, and starts it on the very sample
after the current one ends; it then tells the webview, and the store moves to the same song,
without loading anything again. Every song Rust is given carries a new id, which each of its
events repeats, so an event about a song no longer in the player is ignored. The progress comes
from the sound output's own clock, a few times a second. A song that can't play raises "Couldn't
play" with its title, and the player pauses on it; a sound output lost with no other to move to
raises "Lost the sound output", and the player pauses too.

`windowTitle.ts`, also connected once from `main.tsx`, names the window after the song in the
player, playing or paused, and after the app once it's stopped.

`mediaControls.ts`, connected once from `main.tsx` too, has the keyboard's media keys and the
system's media controls play, pause, stop, skip and seek: Rust reaches them, MPRIS on Linux and
Windows' media controls on Windows, shows them the song in the player, and sends their buttons'
actions to the webview, which hands them to the playback store.

## How the stories run without Tauri

Storybook runs the UI in a plain browser, where there is no Rust side. Three pieces make the stories
work anyway:

- **Fake commands.** `mockCommands` (`src/testing/mockCommands.ts`) answers Rust commands through
  Tauri's own `mockIPC`. Each story mocks the commands its interactions trigger, which also
  documents the commands a component uses. A command that isn't mocked rejects with
  `Unmocked command: <name>`, and `failingCommand(message)` fakes a Rust error. `write_log`, which
  logging runs behind almost every action, is always answered: `loggedMessages()`
  (`src/testing/logs.ts`) lists what a story wrote to the log.
- **Fresh stores.** Before every story, `resetStores` puts every store back to its initial state and
  dismisses every notification, and the logged messages are forgotten. A story then sets the state
  it shows in its `beforeEach`, for example a failed scan. Stories of a component that raises
  notifications render the notification area next to it with the `withNotificationArea` decorator.
- **Drawn covers.** Stories have no songs to read covers from, so `drawCover`
  (`src/testing/covers.ts`) draws a small PNG in the browser for `read_cover` to answer, and
  `noCover` answers a song without one. Every story that shows the song list or the list of
  playlists mocks `read_cover`.
- **One frame per story on Docs pages.** The stores are shared by everything on a page, so stories
  that set store state render in their own iframe there (`renderInOwnFrame`), and don't overwrite
  each other's state.

Stories never connect Rust's player (`songEngine.ts`), the window title (`windowTitle.ts`) or the
media controls (`mediaControls.ts`), so they're silent: the player shows whatever progress and
length the story puts in the store.

The **theme** toolbar switches `data-theme` on `<html>` between `light` and `dark`, like the app's
own toggle does.

## Stories are tests

`pnpm test:ts:storybook` runs every story as a Vitest test in headless Chromium:

- the story must render without errors;
- its `play` function, if any, must pass (e.g. clicking "Try again" brings the songs back);
- the axe accessibility check must find no violations.

The pure logic and the stores have their own unit tests (`*.test.ts`), and the Rust side has its
own tests (`pnpm test:rs`). `pnpm test` runs all of them.
